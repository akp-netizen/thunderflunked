# Faculty Development Game — Rules

Northwest Iowa Community College. A one-hour tabletop + computer hybrid for
faculty development.

## Objective

Each team is an instructional team guiding one fictional college course through a
single semester of five checkpoints. Most players are **Thunderstruck** and want
the course to survive. Exactly one player per table is secretly **Thunderflunked**
and wants it to fail. The training goal is good course-design process — not
identifying "bad students," and not finding one perfect intervention.

The lesson at each checkpoint:
1. **First Week** — Look before reacting.
2. **Early Semester** — Build course systems that can absorb normal change.
3. **First Major Assessment** — Investigate performance before deciding what it means.
4. **Midterm** — Prioritize and scale rather than solving everything individually.
5. **Final Stretch** — Finish sustainably and accurately rather than adding complexity.

## The four resources (0–100)

- **Student Success** — students' ability to engage, persist, respond to
  difficulty, and use support.
- **Course Quality** — health of the learning environment: clarity, alignment,
  accessibility, meaningful practice, feedback, consistency, equitable access.
- **Faculty Capacity** — instructor human bandwidth: attention, judgment,
  communication, follow-up, cognitive/emotional load.
- **Time / Resources** — structural bandwidth: scalable routines, available
  supports, predictable processes, efficient interventions, time.

Status bands: **Normal** 60+, **Strained** 30–59, **Critical** below 30.
Starting resources never begin below **40**. The **Overall Course Score** is a
weakest-link measure (geometric mean) of the four, so one very low resource drags
it down far more than an average would.

### Starting conditions
Your 10-student roster is read as a classroom *system* to set starting resources.
The model uses weighted relevant-stat averages, applies escalating pressure when
low relevant stats cluster, gives diminishing returns for stacking high stats,
and rewards complementary strengths. No single visible stat is a winning drafting
strategy, and the formula is hidden from faculty. **Your Course Card does not
change starting scores.** You also see one non-blaming roster-pattern sentence —
it describes what the group may need, never a per-student penalty.

## Public Roles (perspectives, not powers)

Student Whisperer, Learning Police, Burnout Bouncer, Receipts Department,
Efficiency Gremlin, Professional Buzzkill. Your role is the perspective you are
responsible for bringing into decisions. Roles not drawn at a table are shown as
**Unrepresented Perspectives** — a reminder of a lens no one is formally holding.

## Course Cards

Ten unique courses, two copies each (20-card deck): four lecture-style, two
asynchronous online, four CTE/hands-on. Each has a believable code, credits,
modality, catalog description, meeting/assessment pattern, prerequisite, and
**two Course Realities** — neutral structural facts (e.g., fixed lab dates,
sequential competencies, feedback-heavy grading). Realities do **not** change
starting scores, but they can change how a later action plays out. The team
screen shows each team's Course Card (the original card artwork) and prompts
"Before deciding, review your Course Card" where it matters. Course selection is
fully in the app — nothing is drawn or exchanged on paper.

## Student Cards

Thirty students, each with six visible stats (1–5): **Readiness, Motivation,
Communication, Self-Management, Adaptability, Help-Seeking**, plus a stable
context, strengths, and challenges. Totals run ~16–20; there is no visible total
score. Note the key distinction: *Communication* is "can we have a productive
conversation?" while *Help-Seeking* is "will this student start that conversation
when needed?" (Silent SOS communicates well once approached but almost never
initiates.)

## Course Selection & Trading (fully in the app)

The app deals, trades, and records courses end to end. Flow: players registered →
Role Cards drawn → **the app deals each team one Course Card** from the 20-card
deck → GM **opens** course selection → teams review their card and may propose
**one-for-one** trades with another team *in the app* (one team proposes, the
other accepts; both must agree) → GM **closes** selection → each team's held
course locks in and the student placement begins. A team's course is not locked
until the GM closes selection. Because the deck has two copies of each course,
two teams can hold the same course.

## Student Draft & Rejected-Student Handoff

Each team is dealt **15** Student Cards, drafts exactly **10** for its Starting
Roster, and sets aside **5** Rejected Students. Those 5 are passed physically to
the next team, and each team receives 5 from another team. The software tells each
team exactly which cards to pass, to whom, which to expect, and from whom — no
choosing from 30 and no manual card entry. Received students are held separate
from the roster and do **not** recompute starting resources.

## The five checkpoints

All teams move through checkpoints together under GM control (Open → Lock →
Advance). Each checkpoint presents authored evidence and a decision; decisions
move resources (hidden deltas), and the narrative *shows* consequences rather than
lecturing. A pedagogically good decision does not always raise the Overall Score
immediately — e.g., strong teaching choices often spend Faculty Capacity and Time.

- **CP1 First Week** — up to 3 actions. Includes a planted syllabus-vs-Canvas
  due-date inconsistency (a course problem, not just a student one).
- **CP2 Early Semester** — 2–4 received students join (no immediate score
  movement). Choose up to 2 onboarding actions; over-engineering is possible.
- **CP3 First Major Assessment** — multiple simultaneous causes (engagement *and*
  a practice-to-assessment alignment gap). Up to 3 actions. "Make it easier" is
  not an option.
- **CP4 Midterm** — more needs than you can perfectly address. Choose one
  **priority** and one **supporting** response. If a resource is Critical, the
  "outreach to everyone" option is removed.
- **CP5 Final Stretch** — choose one thing to **Protect**, one to **Repair**, and
  one you will intentionally **Not Add**.

## Resources and student supports (two categories)

Throughout the checkpoints, faculty work with two different kinds of help, and a
core learning objective is telling them apart:

- **Course tools** — things you do *inside* the Canvas course (Dropout Detective,
  Grades/zeros, Message Students Who, Announcements, Modules/Start Here, Rubrics,
  SpeedGrader, Course Analytics, Panorama Accessibility, Simple Syllabus, Grade
  Publish, Course Improvement Survey).
- **Student supports** — real NCC services you *connect a student with*: the
  **Learning Center (Student Success Center)** in Building A (free in-person
  tutoring), **Brainfuse** online tutoring (sidebar link in any Canvas course,
  **15 hours per year**), **TRiO** (free wraparound services for *eligible*
  students only — first-generation, income guidelines, or documented disability),
  **Accessibility / Accommodation Services**, **Academic Advising**, and the
  **NCC Library**.

Both the on-screen course shell (**Student Support & Tools** tab, plus a "Consult
your resources" panel on every decision screen) and the physical **Resource &
Support deck** carry these, labeled COURSE TOOL vs STUDENT SUPPORT.

The pattern the game teaches: **Notice the evidence → decide what *kind* of need it
is (support / course-design / communication / advising-persistence / accessibility)
→ respond at the right scale → use the right tool and/or support → reassess.**
Course **modality matters**: online courses make Brainfuse most accessible,
on-campus courses make the Learning Center natural, and CTE/hands-on courses should
*not* default to tutoring — the evidence may call for practice, clearer directions,
or an accommodation instead. Modality narrows the *most accessible* option; it does
not lock it (online students may use the Learning Center; on-campus students may use
Brainfuse). An unclear-assignment problem is a course issue to fix first, not a
tutoring referral; a class-wide gap is not ten individual referrals.

## Secret Missions

Every player gets one physical Secret Mission, independent of allegiance (the
Thunderflunked may complete missions too). Missions require intentional behavior
(e.g., *get another player to support proactive outreach to a student who hasn't
asked for help*), not things that happen automatically. Some are claimable at
earlier checks, some only at the end. Claiming is private and self-reported on the
device; the software never knows the mission text. Mission completion is only a
small secondary recognition — it does **not** decide the winner.

## Thunderstruck vs. Thunderflunked

- **Thunderstruck:** "You're here to get your students and course through the
  semester."
- **Thunderflunked:** "You're here to make sure they don't." Exactly one per
  table. They know they are alone; everyone else knows only that someone is
  Thunderflunked. Allegiance is assigned privately by the software and revealed to
  each player during the first Private Player Check.

Thunderflunked sabotages mainly through **persuasion**: pushing reasonable ideas
too far, favoring individual fixes over scalable ones, spending capacity on
low-priority issues, over-engineering, quick fixes with later costs, and
occasionally backing a good idea to stay unsuspected. The main danger is the team
*accepting poor decisions* — not the software secretly wrecking the course.

## Emergency Sabotage

The Thunderflunked has **one** optional Emergency Sabotage, available during
Checkpoints 2, 3, or 4; it expires if unused after CP4. It is small (about 2–4
points of pressure or a minor delayed consequence) and can never, by itself,
decide whether the course survives. It is offered **inside the Thunderflunked's
own private turn** at those checkpoints — no separate step — so it does not reveal
who is Thunderflunked. They choose Activate or Save For Later.

## Private Player Check (after every checkpoint, CP1–CP5)

One device per team, passed one player at a time. Each player privately sees their
allegiance (first time only), a Secret Mission claim prompt (which refers to the
player's physical Secret Mission card — the software never shows the mission text),
(Checkpoints 2–4, Thunderflunked only) the Emergency Sabotage option, and a
Suspicion Vote for one other player. Afterward the team sees only a vague report —
suspicion is **scattered**, **concentrated**, or **divided** — never names, counts,
or individual votes. If a session is running long, a facilitator may skip the
private round at CP2 and CP4 (see the run-of-show's 45-minute cut).

## Final Accusation & Reveal

After the CP5 private check, the team sees the final vague suspicion report,
discusses, and accuses **one** player of being Thunderflunked (consensus
preferred; the device accepts whatever single choice is locked in). The app then
reveals allegiance and whether the accusation was correct. **Correct
identification is a secondary achievement and does not change who wins.**

## Win conditions (primary)

- **Thunderstruck win if the course survives. Thunderflunked wins if it fails.**
- The accusation and Secret Missions do **not** decide the primary winner.
- **The course survives if:** Overall Course Score is **at least 58** *and* no
  single resource is below **30**. This is a weakest-link rule — a catastrophic
  resource cannot be hidden by three healthy ones.
- These thresholds are tuned so that thoughtful play survives from any legal
  roster (the roster never predetermines success), sustained poor or passive play
  tends to fail, and Emergency Sabotage only ever nudges a borderline course.

## Final outcome & debrief

Each team sees its course, final Overall Score, all four resources, Survived / Did
Not Survive, the Thunderflunked reveal, whether the accusation was correct, Secret
Mission recognition, and a short semester recap of its major decisions. The game
closes on its core idea:

> Good course design is not about knowing who has the bad idea. It is about having
> a process that catches the bad idea.
